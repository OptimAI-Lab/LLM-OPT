Section 2.1: Unconstrained Optimization#

2.1 Unconstrained Optimization: Definitions and First Principles#

In this chapter, we begin our study of optimization algorithms by focusing on the unconstrained case, where the optimization variable can be chosen from the entire space \(\RR^d\). That is, \(\bw\) can be chosen from the entire \(\RR^d\) space.

Problem Setup#

The general form of an unconstrained optimization problem is:

\[\min_{\vw \in \RR^d} \quad f(\vw)\]

where \(\vw\) is the optimization variable; \(f: \RR^d \to \RR\) is the objective function; There are no explicit constraints on \(\vw\).

Local and Global Optimal Solutions#

Let \(f: \RR^d \to \RR\) be a continuous function.

Definition: Local and Global Optimal Solutions

Let \(f: \RR^d \to \RR\) be a continuous function.

  • Local Minimum: \(\vw^*\) is a local minimizer if there exists \(\epsilon > 0\) such that: $\( f(\vw) \ge f(\vw^*) \quad \forall \vw \in \RR^d \text{ with } \|\vw - \vw^*\| \le \epsilon \)$

  • Global Minimum: \(\hat{\vw}\) is a global minimizer if: $\( f(\vw) \ge f(\hat{\vw}) \quad \forall \vw \in \RR^d \)$

Local optimality describes the function’s behavior in a neighborhood, while global optimality describes the best solution across the entire space.

Existence of Minimizers#

Now let us talk about whether global or local optimal solution exists. Interestingly, e```ven if the objective function is continuous, a minimum may not be attained unless we impose additional structure. For example:

\[\inf_{x \in \RR} \exp(-|x|) = 0\]

But the minimum is never achieved because \(\exp(-|x|) \to 0\) as \(|x| \to \infty\).

A standard result from real analysis helps clarify when minimizers exist:

Weierstrass Theorem

If \(f: \RR^d \to \RR\) is continuous and its level set, defined below:

\[ \{ \vw \in \RR^d : f(\vw) \le f(\vw_0) \} \]

is compact (i.e., closed and bounded) for some point \(\vw_0\), then \(f\) achieves its minimum on \(\RR^d\).

Optimality Conditions#

Now we know when the solutions exist, let’s discuss how to identify them. Towards this end, we will discuss both sufficient and necessary conditions for a point being a local optimal solution.

First-order and second-order necessary condition

If \(f\) is continuously differentiable, and let \(\vw^*\) be a local minimizer. Then the following holds:

\[ \nabla f(\vw^*) = 0 \]

This first-order necessary condition defines a (first-order) stationary points. They are candidates for global/local minmum, maximum solutions, as well as saddle points.

Further, suppose that \(f\) is twice continuously differentiabe. Suppose \(\vw^*\) is a local minimizer, then:

\[ \nabla f(\vw^*) = 0, \quad \nabla^2 f(\vw^*) \succeq 0 \]

This second-order necessary condition defines a (second-order) stationary points. They are candidates for global and local minimum solutions.

Proof (directional-derivative argument)

Let \(d\in\RR^d\) be any direction and define \(g(\alpha)=f(\vw^*+\alpha d)\).
Since \(\vw^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\), \(\alpha=0\) is a local minimizer of \(g\).
Because \(f\) is \(\mathcal C^1\), \(g\) is differentiable and

\[ g'(0)=\nabla f(\vw^*)^\top d. \]

Local minimality of \(g\) at \(0\) implies \(g'(0)=0\). As \(d\) is arbitrary, we must have \(\nabla f(\vw^*)=\vzero\).

Proof (Second-Order Necessary Condition)

If \(\vw^*\) is a local minimizer of a \(\mathcal C^2\) function \(f\), then $\( \nabla f(\vw^*)=\vzero, \qquad \nabla^2 f(\vw^*) \succeq 0. \)$

A Scalar Case Example#

Let \(f: \RR \to \RR\) be twice differentiable. Suppose \(x^*\) satisfies:

\[f'(x^*) = 0, \quad f''(x^*) > 0\]

Then \(x^*\) is a local minimizer. This follows from:

\[f(x) - f(x^*) \approx \frac{1}{2}(x - x^*)^2 f''(x^*), \quad \text{for } x \text{ near } x^*\]

This reasoning generalizes to vector-valued optimization using the Hessian.

Why Are Optimality Conditions Important?#

  • They provide verification tools — necessary conditions rule out non-optimal points; sufficient conditions certify local optima.

  • They are used to design stopping criteria in algorithms: e.g., when \(\|\nabla f(\vw)\| \le \text{tolerance}\).

  • They guide algorithmic design: most algorithms search for points satisfying \(\nabla f(\vw) = 0\) (approximately).

  • In convex problems, first-order conditions are sufficient, making them very powerful. More to come later in subsection [xxxx]
